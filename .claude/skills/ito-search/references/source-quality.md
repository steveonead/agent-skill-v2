# Source Quality

## Keep

- **Primary sources**: official documentation, release notes, changelogs, RFCs, specifications, source code, issues and PRs in the project's own repository
- **Specific and checkable**: version numbers, dates, code, benchmark conditions, or reproduction steps, rather than adjectives
- **Dated**: a publication or last-updated date is visible, and it fits the recorded time range
- **First-hand**: the writer shipped the thing, ran the benchmark, or lived the case, rather than summarizing what other people wrote
- **Attributed**: a named person or organization stands behind the text and carries at least one checkable specific, which is the test that separates real writing from SEO listicles and from unedited LLM output
- **Fit for this claim**: authority is relative to the question, so a strong local newspaper is weak on medicine, and a vendor's own docs are strong on its API and weak on comparisons with rivals

## Drop

- Aggregators and mirrors when the original is reachable, in which case keep the original instead
- Pages whose author gains from the conclusion: comparisons against rivals, affiliate roundups, reviews written by the seller, and testimonials presented as independent testing
- Pages riding a domain that earned its reputation elsewhere, such as third-party commercial content hosted on an established site, or a familiar domain whose ownership and subject both changed
- Pages whose date was refreshed while the content stayed put

## Checks that beat reading the page

- **Read laterally**: what a site says about itself is the weakest available evidence about it, so search the publisher and the author outside their own domain (`[name -site:example.com]`) before leaning on them. Judge a publisher on that off-domain evidence alone, because design polish, a contact page, and the domain suffix are all cheap to fake.
- **Trace to the original**: follow a claim to the source it cites and confirm that source says it. Retellings routinely overstate the study they rest on, and audits of AI research tools find citation accuracy in the 40 to 80 percent range even when every link resolves, so a working link is not evidence of support.
- **Count independent sources, not repetitions**: syndication, content farms, and link farms multiply one claim across many pages, and repetition alone shifts belief toward whatever was repeated. Two findings corroborate each other only when neither is downstream of the other.
- **Judge suspected machine-written pages at site level**: the durable signals are structural, namely a byline with no existence off the site, publishing volume no human could sustain, one owner operating dozens of near-identical domains, the same templated sections on every page, and citations that fail to resolve. Individual tells such as em dashes or a fashionable vocabulary turn over with each model generation and misfire on careful human writing.

## Ranking within what remains

When surviving findings conflict, the one closer to the primary source wins, and among equals the more recent one wins. A conflict that survives both tests is reported as an open disagreement, named on both sides.

## Language

Sources in any language other than Simplified Chinese are fair game, and translate what you quote.
